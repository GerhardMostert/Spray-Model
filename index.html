How crop spraying works

Read this first

Before the numbers make sense, it helps to know what the aeroplane is actually doing and what the pilot controls. Six things decide where the spray ends up: height, swath, nozzle type, boom pressure, airspeed and wind.

01 — The job

Parallel passes

The aircraft flies straight passes back and forth across the field a few metres above the crop, releasing spray from a boom under the wings. Each pass treats a band called the swath — for a light ag aircraft that is usually 18–24 m wide. The passes are spaced so they just meet, using a GPS lightbar or autopilot.

A 100 ha field at a 24 m swath is roughly 42 km of flying, in about forty passes.

02 — Height

Why 3 m matters

Height above the crop is the single biggest thing the pilot controls. Everything released has to fall that distance before it lands, and the whole time it is falling the wind is pushing it sideways.

Fly at 3 m instead of 6 m and every droplet spends half as long in the air, so it travels roughly half as far sideways. Flying low is not bravado — it is the main drift control.

03 — Nozzles

CP-11TT and pressure

The CP-11TT is a flat-fan nozzle with a selectable deflector plate. Three settings decide droplet size: the orifice, the boom pressure in psi, and the deflection angle (5°, 10°, 15°, 30°). Airspeed matters too — faster air shears the sheet into finer droplets.

More pressure means finer droplets and more drift. A bigger deflection angle means finer droplets and more drift. At 5° and 60 psi at around 130 mph, a CP-11TT sits in the Very Coarse band — roughly 400–450 µm.

04 — Droplet size

The whole argument

Drift is a droplet-size question and nothing else. A 400 µm droplet falls at about 1.6 m/s, so from 3 m it lands in under two seconds. A 100 µm droplet falls at about 0.25 m/s and takes twelve. Same wind, five times the sideways travel.

And volume goes with the cube of diameter: one 400 µm droplet carries as much chemical as 64 droplets of 100 µm. That is why the fine fraction, which travels furthest, carries so little of the load.

05 — Weather window

Wind and Delta T

The working window is a steady 5–15 km/h across the swath, blowing in a known direction. Dead calm is worse than a light breeze, because dead calm usually means a temperature inversion, and an inversion holds the fine fraction in a layer that can drift a long way intact.

Delta T (dry bulb minus wet bulb) should sit between about 2 and 8. Above 10 the droplets shrink fast in flight and start behaving like much smaller droplets than the ones that left the nozzle.

06 — What drift is

Swath is not drift

The swath is the band you are deliberately treating. Drift is only the fraction light enough to still be airborne when it leaves that band. With a Very Coarse setting that is a couple of percent of the tank, not the load.

If the last pass is flown with its edge right on the fence, then a crosswind pushes part of the actual swath over the line. That is placement, not drift — and it is why a half-swath buffer on a boundary pass changes the outcome so much.

Why a whole field claim must be compared with the affected strip

Deposit past a fence does not spread out evenly across the neighbour's field — it collapses. The heavy droplets, which carry nearly all the chemical, land within the swath or a few metres past it. Only the light fraction keeps going, and it is a small fraction carrying a tiny share of the load.

Run the model below and watch the numbers: with a Very Coarse setting at 3 m, the deposit at the fence is high, at 25 m it is already under one percent of the applied rate, and by 100 m it is a few hundredths of a percent. A 100 ha field with a 1 km shared boundary is a kilometre deep. For the far corner to take a dose that marks a crop, the deposit a kilometre out would have to be within a hundredfold of the deposit at the fence. It is not — it is thousands of times lower.

Push the wind slider up as far as it goes. Even at speeds nobody would legally or safely spray in, the strip that reaches a damaging dose is measured in tens of metres, not kilometres. Drift from a boundary pass affects a strip along the fence, and it always leaves a gradient — heavy at the line, fading fast. A field that is uniformly affected right to the far corner was not done by droplet drift from a boundary pass: that pattern points at something else, such as a temperature inversion carrying a concentrated fine cloud, or vapour movement of a volatile herbicide after it has landed.

What the model is doing

So it can be checked

Droplet spectrum

Pressure, deflection angle and airspeed give a volume median diameter, then a log-normal volume distribution around it with a realistic relative span, plus a small fine tail — because real nozzles always produce a few percent of fines even on the coarsest setting.

Fall and travel

Each droplet size is flown down from the release height using its true terminal velocity (Schiller–Naumann drag, solved iteratively) through a logarithmic wind profile referenced to the 2 m wind. Droplets shrink as they fall, at a rate set by Delta T, so small ones slow down and travel further — the model does not flatter the operator here.

Spreading and landing

The swath is treated as a uniform line source of the set width. Each size class lands displaced downwind and smeared by turbulence — the spread grows with travel distance. Anything still airborne after five minutes is counted as a dispersed fraction and spread as a long, very thin tail rather than dropped from the books.

What this page is and isn't

Read this before you show it to anyone

  • This is an illustrative model built to explain the physics. It is not AGDISP or AgDRIFT, the validated models used in regulatory and expert work, and it has not been calibrated against field trials.
  • Droplet spectra are generated from a pressure–deflection–airspeed relationship, not from measured atomisation data for your specific CP-11TT orifice, setting and tank mix.
  • It assumes steady wind, flat ground, a neutral atmosphere and no inversion. A temperature inversion changes the answer completely and is a common cause of long-distance movement.
  • It does not model volatilisation of the product after it lands, or vapour drift — which for some herbicides matters more than droplet drift, and which can affect a much larger area than droplet drift ever will.
  • Actual affected area is established by evidence, not simulation: the damage pattern in the crop, tissue residue sampling, your job sheet, and on-site weather records for that day.
  • Use this to explain the concept. For the claim itself, get an independent agronomist or drift specialist to assess the field, and speak to your insurer and a lawyer before you present anything as a finding.
